Reliability & Operating in Production // 01

Resilience Patterns

Scaling & Load Balancing ended on an uncomfortable fact: a load balancer's health check always has a detection window, and requests routed to a dying backend during that window fail or hang. That's not a flaw in health checking — it's a structural limit of any mechanism that reacts to failure instead of bounding it. This page is about the other half of the story: what the caller does so that a failing dependency — a slow database, a flaky third-party API, one bad instance the load balancer hasn't evicted yet — costs it a bounded amount of pain instead of an unbounded amount. Every pattern here is really one idea applied at different scopes: fail fast, contain the damage, and don't make things worse for the failing dependency or for the parts of your system that don't depend on it.

★ Insight

Resilience patterns don't make dependencies more reliable — nothing you do in your service fixes someone else's outage. What they do is convert a dependency's failure from your outage into a contained, bounded, visible degradation of your own service. That reframing — "I cannot prevent downstream failure, only bound my exposure to it" — is the mental model this whole page builds from.

Timeouts: the one number every network call must have

Networking & Protocols called "the network is not reliable" the first fallacy of distributed computing. Its direct consequence is this: a network call without a timeout doesn't fail — it hangs, for as long as the OS-level TCP timeout takes to give up, which can be minutes. In Concurrency, Threads & Async I/O you saw why that's lethal in a thread-per-request pool: a worker blocked waiting on a hung call is a worker that can't serve anything else, and enough hung calls exhaust the pool — new requests queue, then time out themselves, and a slow *dependency* becomes a *total* outage of a service that doesn't even need that dependency for most of its traffic.

The fix sounds trivial and is: every network call gets an explicit timeout, chosen to be just above the dependency's real p99 latency, not "whatever the client library defaults to" (often: none). Two refinements matter in practice:

  • Timeout budgets compose. If service A calls B which calls C, A's total timeout has to be larger than B's, which has to be larger than C's — otherwise A gives up and retries while B is still legitimately waiting on C, doubling load on a struggling C for no benefit. A deadline passed down the call chain (a remaining-time header, `context.Context` in Go, a gRPC deadline) is the disciplined version: each hop computes its own timeout as "time budget left," not a fixed constant.
  • Connect timeout ≠ read/request timeout. Failing to establish a TCP connection (dependency is down) should fail fast — a couple hundred milliseconds. Waiting for a response after the connection is open can legitimately take longer for some endpoints. Conflating the two either makes you too slow to notice a dead host or too quick to abandon a slow-but-alive one.
! Pitfall

A timeout that's too tight is its own failure mode: it fires on requests that would have succeeded a moment later, turning "slow" into "failed" and — if paired with retries — multiplying load on a dependency that was actually fine, just briefly at the tail of its latency distribution. Set timeouts from measured p99 (or p99.9) latency, not a round number picked by feel; Performance, Profiling & Capacity Planning, later in this section, is where "what's the real tail" gets measured properly.

Retries: turning permanent failures into transient ones — carefully

Plenty of failures are transient: a packet drops, a server briefly GCs, a load-balancer health check hasn't caught up yet. Retrying is often the right response — but a retry is a second request, and the naive version, retry-immediately-forever, is one of the most common ways backend engineers accidentally cause the outage they were trying to survive.

! Pitfall — the retry storm

Picture a downstream service that's degraded — succeeding, but slowly, at maybe 60% of normal throughput. Every caller times out, retries immediately, and now the downstream is receiving the original load plus a wave of retries — which pushes it further past its capacity, causing more timeouts, causing more retries. This is a retry storm (a specific, self-reinforcing case of the thundering-herd problem you also saw in Caching Strategies's cache stampede): the caller's own recovery attempt is the thing that keeps the downstream down. Retries without restraint don't add resilience — they add a positive feedback loop.

Three disciplines turn a dangerous retry into a safe one:

Exponential backoff
Wait longer between each successive retry — typically base × 2^attempt (100ms, 200ms, 400ms, 800ms…), capped at some maximum. This spreads retries out in time instead of hammering the downstream at the exact moment it's struggling most.
Jitter
Backoff alone still synchronizes callers: if a thousand clients all failed at the same instant (say, the downstream just came back from an outage), pure exponential backoff has all thousand retry at the same moments — 100ms later, 200ms later, in lockstep. Adding randomness (jitter) to each wait — e.g. a random value in [0, backoff] — desynchronizes them, spreading the retry wave out instead of concentrating it into further synchronized spikes. "Full jitter" (retry after a uniformly random delay up to the exponential cap) is the AWS-popularized default for good reason: it has the lowest worst-case load on the downstream of the common strategies.
A retry budget (cap the total, not just per-request)
Bound both the number of attempts per request (2–3 is typical — more rarely helps and multiplies tail latency) and the fraction of a service's total outbound traffic that's allowed to be retries (e.g. "retries may not exceed 10% of requests in any 10s window"). The per-request cap bounds one client's damage; the budget bounds the fleet's aggregate damage, which is what actually prevents a storm at scale.
! Pitfall — retries need idempotency

A retry means "maybe send this request again," which is only safe if sending it twice has the same effect as sending it once. Designing Clean REST APIs covered exactly this: a non-idempotent POST /payments retried blindly can double-charge a customer, because the first attempt may have actually succeeded server-side and only the response was lost to the timeout. Safe retries require either a naturally idempotent operation (a PUT that sets absolute state) or an idempotency key — a client-generated ID the server deduplicates on, so a retried request with the same key is recognized and returns the original result instead of repeating the side effect. This is the same mechanism Distributed Transactions, Sagas & Idempotency uses to get "effective exactly-once" out of at-least-once delivery — it's not a coincidence; retries are the local, single-hop version of the same problem messaging systems solve at the fleet scale.

Circuit breakers: stop calling what's already broken

Timeouts bound how long you wait for a single call; retries decide whether to try again. Neither one asks the more useful question when a dependency has been failing for a while: should we even try? A circuit breaker tracks a dependency's recent success/failure rate and, once it crosses a threshold, stops sending it traffic entirely for a cooldown period — failing every call immediately, without waiting for a timeout, without consuming a worker or a connection. It's named for the electrical breaker it mimics: trip it once the current (failures) gets dangerous, and nothing downstream draws power until it's reset.

The state machine is three states, and it's worth knowing the transitions precisely — this is a favorite "explain it precisely" interview probe:

StateBehaviorTransitions on
Closed (normal) Calls pass through to the dependency; failures are counted Failure rate (or consecutive failures) crosses a threshold → Open
Open (tripped) Calls fail immediately — no network call is made at all Cooldown timer expires → Half-open
Half-open (probing) A small number of trial calls are allowed through to test recovery Trials succeed → Closed; trials fail → back to Open (cooldown restarts)

The payoff is symmetric: the caller stops paying the (often multi-second) timeout cost for calls that were going to fail anyway, freeing its own worker pool for requests that don't need the broken dependency; and the struggling dependency stops receiving load it can't handle, which is often exactly what it needs to actually recover. A circuit breaker is, in effect, an automatic, self-tuning version of the load-shedding decision below, scoped to one dependency.

i Note

Circuit breakers are usually scoped per dependency, per instance (sometimes per downstream host) — a breaker for "calls to the recommendations service" trips independently of one for "calls to the payments service," so one bad dependency doesn't silence calls to a healthy one. Libraries like resilience4j, Polly, or a service mesh's built-in breaker (Envoy/Istio outlier detection) implement this bookkeeping so you don't hand-roll the failure-counting logic yourself.

Bulkheads: containing the blast radius

Named after a ship's watertight compartments: a hull breach in one compartment floods that compartment, not the whole ship, because bulkheads physically wall them off. Applied to a backend service, a bulkhead means partitioning shared resources — most commonly a worker/thread pool or a connection pool — so that one dependency's failure can exhaust only its own partition, not the resources every other endpoint also needs.

Without a bulkhead, a single shared pool means the scenario Concurrency, Threads & Async I/O and Anatomy of a Backend Service both flagged as cascading failure: a slow call to Dependency X ties up workers that Endpoint Y — which has nothing to do with X — also needed, and Y goes down purely from resource starvation, with X's outage as the root cause. Reserve a fixed slice of the pool (or a fixed-size connection pool) for calls to X, sized to X's realistic worst case, and Y's requests can never be starved by X no matter how badly X fails.

! Caution

Bulkheads trade throughput for isolation: a reserved pool sized for X's worst case sits partly idle most of the time, capacity that a fully shared pool could have lent to Y during X's quiet periods. That's the price of the guarantee — decide it's worth paying for dependencies whose failure would otherwise take down unrelated functionality, and skip it for low-stakes calls where a shared pool's efficiency matters more than isolation.

Load shedding and graceful degradation

Timeouts, retries, breakers, and bulkheads all manage calls to a dependency. Load shedding manages the service's own incoming traffic when total demand exceeds what it can serve — whether the cause is a traffic spike or reduced capacity from a degraded dependency eating into the pool. Two related but distinct moves:

Load shedding
Once a queue or pool passes a threshold, actively reject new requests (a fast 503, ideally with a Retry-After) rather than accept them and let them queue indefinitely. It looks like giving up on some users, and it is — deliberately: finishing the requests you've already accepted well is better than accepting everything and finishing none of it in time. Priority matters here: shed low-priority or non-critical traffic (background jobs, low-tier customers, prefetch requests) first, before touching the traffic that matters most.
Graceful degradation
Under pressure, serve a cheaper, partial response instead of the full one rather than failing outright: return a product page without personalized recommendations if the recommendation service is down, serve a cached (possibly stale) result instead of a fresh one if the primary store is slow, skip a non-essential enrichment step. The user gets something correct-if-incomplete instead of an error page.

Both require deciding, ahead of time and by product judgment (not purely engineering), which parts of a response are essential and which are nice-to-have — that classification is what makes shedding and degradation safe rather than arbitrary.

How the patterns compose

PatternProtects againstCosts
TimeoutUnbounded waits on a hung callToo tight → false failures on slow-but-fine calls
Retry (+ backoff/jitter)Transient, one-off failuresExtra load on the dependency; unsafe without idempotency; storms without backoff+budget
Circuit breakerWasting resources on a dependency that's been failing for a whileFalse trips on brief blips if the threshold is too sensitive; requests fail fast (not succeed) while open
BulkheadOne dependency's failure starving unrelated requestsReserved capacity sits idle when not needed — lower utilization
Load sheddingA queue growing without bound under overloadDeliberately drops some requests — needs a priority policy
Graceful degradationAn optional dependency's failure becoming a full outageRequires upfront design of what's essential vs optional

In production these stack: a timeout bounds each attempt, a retry budget with backoff+jitter governs whether and how to try again, a circuit breaker short-circuits the whole dance once the dependency is clearly down, a bulkhead makes sure that dance can't starve unrelated traffic while it plays out, and load shedding is the last line of defense if, despite all of that, total demand still exceeds what the service can do. None of them is a substitute for the others — a circuit breaker without a bulkhead still starves the pool during the window before it trips; a bulkhead without a timeout just isolates the damage of a hang instead of preventing it.

★ ML-systems bridge

An inference gateway calling a feature store, a vector DB, or a second model in a pipeline (retrieval → rerank → generate) is exactly this picture. A hung feature-store call without a timeout stalls the request thread and, without a bulkhead, starves capacity for requests that didn't need that feature at all — the same cascading failure, just with "GPU worker" in place of "thread." Circuit breakers are common around optional enrichment calls (fall back to a default feature vector or a smaller model if the primary is unhealthy, rather than failing the whole request) — a form of graceful degradation tailored to serving. And retries need the same idempotency discipline: retrying a non-deterministic generation call can produce a different output on the second attempt, which is a correctness issue disguised as a reliability fix if the caller assumes retried calls are interchangeable.

1. A downstream dependency is degraded — succeeding, but at 60% of normal throughput. All callers retry immediately on timeout, with no backoff. What happens?

Retries are additional requests. Without backoff and jitter to spread them out, retrying immediately on a struggling dependency adds load exactly when it can least afford it, which is what defines a retry storm.

2. A circuit breaker for "calls to the recommendations service" is OPEN. What happens when a request that needs a recommendation arrives?

That's the entire point of the open state: skip the network call and its timeout entirely, fail fast, and free the worker — both sparing the caller the wait and sparing the struggling dependency the load.

3. Order Service and Profile Service share one worker pool. Order Service's downstream dependency is hanging (calls take 5s each, well above what the pool can absorb at the incoming rate). Profile Service has nothing to do with that dependency. Which single pattern most directly prevents Profile Service's requests from also failing?

Timeouts and circuit breakers reduce how much of the pool Order Service's failing calls consume, but only a bulkhead structurally guarantees Profile Service can't be starved by them — it partitions the shared resource so one dependency's failure is contained to its own slice.
