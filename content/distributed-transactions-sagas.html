Distributed Systems & Scaling // 05

Distributed Transactions, Sagas & Idempotency

A single Postgres transaction gives you something remarkable: a set of writes that either all happen or none do, even if the process crashes mid-way, covered in full in Transactions, ACID & Isolation Levels. The moment those writes span two services — an order service, a payment service, an inventory service, each with its own database — that guarantee is gone, and nothing you can buy or configure brings it back for free. This page is about the gap between "I want this to be atomic" and "atomic across a network is a different, much harder problem," and the two ways production systems actually close it: pay for stronger coordination up front (two-phase commit), or accept it's not truly atomic and design so that's survivable (sagas). It's also the question senior backend interviews reach for almost every time system design touches more than one service: "how do you handle a distributed transaction without 2PC?"

Why cross-service ACID doesn't exist for free

Say checkout needs to: deduct inventory, charge a card, and create a shipment record — each owned by a different service with its own database, the way horizontal scaling and service ownership push you to split things. A naive implementation calls each service in turn: 

reserve_inventory(order)   // service A commits
charge_card(order)         // service B commits
create_shipment(order)     // service C crashes before this call

Inventory is reserved, the card is charged, and the process died before shipment was ever created. There's no rollback button — the two prior commits are already durable, in two other databases you don't control from here. This is exactly the dual-write problem from the messaging page, generalized from "one database + one broker" to "N independently-committing services." Each individual step is ACID; the sequence of them is not, because nothing wraps all N commits in a single all-or-nothing unit.

★ Insight

Every technique on this page is really answering one question: who decides, and when, that a multi-step operation spanning independent systems is "done"? A local transaction answers it with a single log record the database writes atomically. Once you cross a network boundary, that single point of truth doesn't exist — so you either simulate one with a coordinator that a majority of participants trust (2PC), or you give up on a single moment of truth entirely and make every intermediate state a valid, recoverable state of the world (sagas). There's no third option that's actually free.

Two-phase commit: the classic answer, and its cost

Two-phase commit (2PC) is the textbook way to make a distributed transaction atomic. A coordinator asks every participant to prepare, and only tells everyone to commit once all have said yes:

Phase 1 — Prepare (vote)
The coordinator asks every participant "can you commit?" Each participant does whatever work is needed to guarantee it can commit on request — validates constraints, acquires locks, writes its intended change to a durable log — then replies yes (prepared) or no. Critically, a participant that votes yes must be able to commit later no matter what happens to it in between (crash, restart) — that's the guarantee the whole protocol rests on.
Phase 2 — Commit (decide)
If every participant voted yes, the coordinator logs the decision and tells everyone to commit for real. If even one voted no (or timed out), the coordinator tells everyone to abort, and each participant rolls back the work it provisionally did in phase 1.
! Pitfall — the blocking problem

A participant that has voted yes is now obligated to hold its locks and wait — it cannot unilaterally commit or abort, because it doesn't know what the other participants voted. If the coordinator crashes after collecting votes but before broadcasting the decision, every prepared participant is stuck holding locks indefinitely, blocking any other transaction that touches the same rows, until the coordinator recovers and tells them what happened. This is 2PC's defining weakness: it turns a coordinator outage into a cluster-wide locking incident, which is precisely the kind of "one slow/down dependency takes everything else down with it" failure mode that the rest of this roadmap tries to design away from.

2PC also assumes every participant speaks the protocol and stays reachable for the duration — fine inside one database's internal transaction manager, or across a small number of systems you tightly control (some XA-compliant deployments still do this), but it doesn't compose well with services owned by other teams, third-party APIs you don't control the internals of, or a network with real, un-bounded latency. That combination — blocking failure mode, poor fit for service boundaries, and every participant paying a latency and availability tax on every transaction — is why 2PC has largely fallen out of favor for inter-service transactions in modern backends, even though it's still exactly correct where it's used (many single-database engines use a 2PC-like protocol internally across their own replicas/shards, where the "coordinator" is trusted infrastructure, not another team's service).

The saga pattern

A saga gives up on true atomicity and replaces it with something weaker but achievable: break the operation into a sequence of local transactions, each in its own service, and define a compensating action for each step that can undo its effect if a later step fails. Nothing is ever locked waiting on a remote decision — every step commits for real, immediately, in its own service. If step 4 of 6 fails, the saga runs compensations for steps 3, 2, and 1 in reverse, so the system ends up in a consistent state again — just not the same path it would take if everything succeeded.

Two-phase commitSaga
Isolation True — nothing is externally visible until the final commit. None between steps — each local transaction commits and is immediately visible. Other requests can observe an order as "paid" before "shipped" exists.
Failure recovery Automatic rollback via the protocol; but the coordinator can leave participants blocked holding locks. Compensating actions you must design and write yourself, per step — there's no generic "undo."
Availability Lower — every participant must stay reachable and hold locks for the duration of the round trip. Higher — each step is a normal, fast local transaction; no cross-service locks held at all.
Reversibility Rollback undoes a provisional change that was never externally visible. Compensation reverses an already-committed, possibly already-observed effect — some effects (an email sent, an SMS) can't truly be undone, only apologized for.
! Caution — sagas are not isolated

The loss of isolation is the trade-off people underestimate. Between "inventory reserved" and "payment charged," another process reading the order sees an intermediate, possibly-doomed state. If that saga later fails and compensates, anyone who acted on the intermediate state (sent a "your order is confirmed!" push notification, let a second customer see the item as available again mid-reservation) has to live with having reacted to something that got undone. Real saga implementations often need their own countermeasures for this — e.g. a "pending" status that downstream consumers know not to treat as final — which is extra design surface 2PC's isolation would have hidden for you.

Orchestration vs. choreography

There are two ways to wire up which service decides what happens next:

Orchestration
A dedicated orchestrator (or the initiating service) explicitly calls each participant in sequence and tells it what to do — including which compensations to trigger on failure. The saga's logic lives in one place, as an explicit state machine, which makes it easy to see the whole flow, add steps, and reason about "what happens if step 3 fails" by reading one file. The cost is a central component that every participant now depends on, and that component needs its own durability (if the orchestrator crashes mid-saga, it must resume from where it left off, not restart from scratch or forget the saga entirely).
Choreography
There is no central coordinator — each service reacts to events from the others (exactly the event-driven architecture machinery: topics, consumers, at-least-once delivery). OrderCreated triggers inventory to reserve stock and emit InventoryReserved, which triggers payment to charge and emit PaymentCharged, and so on; a failure emits a *Failed event that upstream services listen for to run their own compensation. This avoids a single dependency everyone calls through, but the overall flow is now implicit, scattered across services' event handlers — nobody's code shows you the whole saga, and debugging "why did this order get stuck half-compensated" means reconstructing the story from logs across N services.
✓ Tip

Choreography scales better organizationally for a handful of steps with genuinely independent participants, but it degrades fast as steps grow — a 6-step saga in pure choreography is 6 services each holding a fragment of a state machine nobody can see whole. Most teams that start choreographed converge on orchestration once a saga passes roughly 4-5 steps or gains a step that needs to know "how far did we get" (e.g. a timeout that should trigger compensation) — which is really just re-deriving the case for an explicit state machine over an implicit, distributed one, the same lesson that shows up anywhere business logic threatens to become implicit (stored procedures, deeply nested event chains, magic ORM callbacks).

Idempotency: what makes retries and compensation safe

Sagas (and 2PC recovery, and message redelivery) all depend on being able to retry a step without fear — a network timeout doesn't tell you whether the request landed, so the only safe move is often "try again," and trying again must not double-charge a card or double-reserve stock. This is the same idempotency concept from idempotent consumers and from idempotency keys on REST APIs, and it's the load-bearing property underneath every technique on this page:

  • Idempotency keys — the caller generates a unique key per logical operation (e.g. charge:order-42) and sends it with every attempt, including retries. The receiving service records which keys it has already completed and, on a repeat, returns the original result instead of re-executing — the same request replayed twice produces one effect.
  • Compensating actions must themselves be idempotent — a compensation can also be retried (the service issuing it can crash and retry too), so "release the reservation" must be safe to call twice, e.g. by checking current state before acting, or by making the release an absolute set rather than a relative decrement.
  • Effectively-once, not exactly-once — as with messaging, there's no free exactly- once here either. The honest, achievable target is at-least-once execution + an idempotent operation, which behaves like exactly-once from the outside even though the underlying call may physically happen more than once.
  • Semantic vs. compensating undo — some actions have no meaningful compensation at all (an email already read, an SMS already delivered). Design the saga so irreversible side effects happen last, after every step that's still cheaply compensable has succeeded — a card charge (reversible via refund) should generally precede "send confirmation email" (not reversible), not the other way around.
! Pitfall — compensation without idempotency is a new bug, not a fix

A team that adds compensation logic but forgets it needs the same idempotency discipline as the forward steps just relocates the bug: now a retried compensation double-refunds a card, or a redelivered "release inventory" message releases stock that a different saga already re-reserved in the meantime. Compensation is still a distributed operation subject to at-least-once delivery — it doesn't get to skip the rules the forward path had to follow.

★ ML-systems bridge

A training pipeline that has to update a feature store, kick off a training job, and register a new model version is a saga whether or not anyone calls it that. If model registration fails after the feature store was already updated and a costly training run already completed, you don't want to silently leave a half-updated feature store or an orphaned training run burning GPU-hours for nothing — you want an explicit compensating step (roll back the feature-store version, tag the training run as abandoned) the same way an order saga rolls back inventory. And "resume this pipeline from where it crashed" only works safely if each stage is idempotent — re-running a training job that already completed and registered a model, because a retry fired after a timeout, is the training- pipeline version of double-charging a card. The orchestration-vs-choreography choice maps directly too: an explicit DAG orchestrator (Airflow, Argo, a custom state machine) is the "orchestration" saga; a chain of pipeline stages each triggered by the previous stage's completion event is "choreography," with the exact same trade-off — visibility and central control vs. loose coupling that gets hard to debug once the DAG grows.

1. A coordinator running two-phase commit collects "yes" votes from all three participants, then crashes before sending the commit decision. What happens to the participants, and why is this 2PC's defining weakness?

A participant that has voted "yes" has given up the right to decide unilaterally — it must be ready to commit on request no matter what, which means it can't safely guess abort either (the coordinator might have decided commit). That forces it to block, holding locks, until the coordinator recovers and tells it the decision — the exact failure mode that makes 2PC a poor fit across independently-owned services.

2. In a saga, step 4 of 5 fails after steps 1-3 already committed locally. What is the correct response, and what property must each compensating action have?

A saga's whole point is that local commits along the way are real and visible, so a later failure has to be undone explicitly via compensating actions run in reverse — and because compensation is itself just another distributed call subject to retries and at-least-once delivery, it needs the same idempotency discipline as the forward path, or a retried compensation can cause a new bug (e.g. double-refunding).

3. A 6-step order saga is implemented via choreography: each service reacts to the previous service's event and emits its own. The team wants to add a timeout that triggers compensation if the saga stalls partway through. What's the practical difficulty this reveals, and what's the common fix?

Choreography's implicit, distributed state is exactly what makes "has this saga stalled, and where" hard to answer — no service holds the whole picture. That's the classic pressure that pushes teams from choreography to orchestration as saga complexity grows: an explicit state machine can track progress and own timeout/compensation logic in one visible place, at the cost of a central dependency.
