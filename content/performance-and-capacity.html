Reliability & Operating in Production // 03

Performance, Profiling & Capacity Planning

Observability gave you the instruments to see that something is wrong and roughly where. This page is about the numbers behind those instruments: what latency and throughput actually mean and how they trade off, why the average is close to useless for anything user-facing, why a single slow dependency gets worse the more services a request fans out to, and how to turn "will this handle launch day" into an estimate you can defend with arithmetic instead of a shrug. It's the quantitative half of production-readiness — the half that lets you answer "how much capacity do we actually have" before the answer arrives as an incident.

Latency vs. throughput: not the same axis

Latency is how long one request takes. Throughput is how many requests the system completes per unit time. They're related but not interchangeable, and conflating them is a common planning mistake:

  • You can raise throughput while making latency worse — batching is the classic move. Group 32 requests into one GPU inference call and you 10x throughput per GPU-second, but each individual request now waits for the batch to fill, so its latency goes up. (More on this in the Concurrency & Async I/O page's thread-pool-vs-event-loop trade-off, and again in the ML bridge below.)
  • You can improve latency while capping throughput — give every request its own dedicated thread and CPU core and each one runs as fast as the hardware allows, right up until you run out of cores, at which point throughput collapses because nothing new can start.
  • Both are governed by the same underlying resource: concurrency (how much work is in flight at once) — which is exactly what Little's Law, below, makes precise.

A system is never described by one number. "Fast" without a throughput figure, or "handles 10k req/s" without a latency figure, is an incomplete sentence — and interviewers who ask "how would this scale" are usually listening for whether you reach for both.

Why the average lies: percentiles and the shape of a distribution

Request latency is never a single number repeated — it's a distribution, usually with a long right tail: most requests are fast, a minority are slow, and a few are very slow (GC pause, a lock contended, a cold cache, a retry). The mean gets dragged toward that tail and hides exactly the thing you need to see:

! Pitfall — "average latency is 45ms" can still mean 1 in 100 users waits 3 seconds

Take 1,000 requests: 990 complete in ~20ms and 10 take 3,000ms (a GC pause, say). The mean is (990×20 + 10×3000) / 1000 ≈ 49.8ms — looks fine. But that "fine" number is a lie for the 1% who experienced 3 seconds, and at scale 1% is not a rounding error: at 10,000 req/s, 1% is 100 people every second having a bad experience your dashboard is telling you doesn't exist. This is why SRE practice reports percentiles — p50 (median), p95, p99, sometimes p99.9 — instead of, or alongside, the mean. p99 = 3000ms in that example tells the truth the mean hid.

Percentiles matter more, not less, as a service grows, for a structural reason: tail latency amplification. If a single call has a 1% chance of hitting the slow path, a user who triggers 20 such calls per page load (a common fan-out — auth, several backend services, a couple of DB queries each) has a much higher chance that at least one of them is slow:

P(at least one slow call in N) = 1 − (1 − p)N

At p = 1% and N = 20: 1 − 0.99^20 ≈ 18%. Nearly one in five page loads now experiences the "rare" slow path, purely because of fan-out — nobody's code got slower, the composition did. This is precisely the mechanism the widget below lets you drive with your own numbers, and it's why "our p99 is fine" at the single-service level is not the same claim as "our users have a good p99" once you compose enough calls per request.

Little's Law: the equation behind capacity

Little's Law is a small, exact result from queueing theory that applies to almost every backend capacity question: in a stable system, the average number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the average time each spends in the system.

L = λ × W

L — concurrency
Average number of requests being worked on at once (in the app, in a queue, on a connection pool) — this is the number that determines how many threads, connections, or pool slots you need.
λ (lambda) — arrival rate / throughput
Requests arriving (and, at steady state, completing) per unit time — req/s.
W — latency
Average time a request spends in the system, from arrival to completion.

This is the formal version of the thread-pool-sizing intuition from Concurrency, Threads & Async I/O: if you're serving 2,000 req/s and each takes 50ms on average, you need roughly 2000 × 0.05 = 100 requests' worth of concurrency in flight at any moment — 100 threads (or 100 outstanding async tasks) is your floor, not a guess. Raise λ or W without raising available concurrency and the excess doesn't vanish — it queues, which is exactly how W then gets worse, a feedback loop that turns "a bit slower" into a queue that never drains once utilization crosses the point where arrivals outpace completions.

✓ Tip — use it in both directions

Little's Law is symmetric, and each direction answers a different real question: given expected traffic (λ) and a latency budget (W), it tells you the pool size (L) to provision. Given a fixed pool size (L) and a measured latency (W), it tells you the maximum throughput (λ) that pool can sustain before requests start queueing — a quick sanity check on "why is this connection pool the bottleneck" during an incident, no load test required.

Back-of-the-envelope capacity estimation

Interviewers ask "how would you estimate scale" because it's precisely the skill that separates "I'll provision generously and hope" from a defensible number. The method is the same every time — start from a business number, derive load, then derive resource requirements — worked here for a hypothetical URL-shortener-style service:

StepReasoningResult
1. Business scale 10M daily active users, each creates ~0.2 links/day and clicks ~2 links/day 2M writes/day, 20M reads/day
2. Average QPS 22M requests over 86,400 seconds/day ~255 req/s average
3. Peak QPS Traffic isn't uniform — apply a peak factor (commonly 3–5× average for diurnal traffic; higher for a launch or viral spike) ~250 × 4 ≈ 1,000 req/s peak
4. Storage 2M new rows/day × ~500 bytes/row (URL + metadata + index overhead) × 365 × 5 years ~1.8 TB over 5 years — modest, fits a single well-indexed relational store
5. Bandwidth Peak reads (~900 req/s of the 1,000) × ~1 KB average response ~0.9 MB/s peak egress — trivial; caching will absorb most of it anyway
6. Concurrency (Little's Law) Peak QPS × p99 latency budget: 1,000 req/s × 0.05s ~50 requests in flight at peak — sizes the app-tier pool

Notice what the exercise actually produces: not a precise number (nobody believes the "5th significant figure" of a back-of-envelope estimate) but the right order of magnitude and, just as importantly, which resource is the actual constraint. Here, reads dominate writes 10:1 — that alone tells you caching (Caching Strategies) is probably where the design effort belongs, before you've written a line of code. Getting the order of magnitude right — thousands vs. millions of req/s — is the bar; spending an hour trying to nail the exact figure is a waste the exercise doesn't reward.

Load testing: finding the wall before your users do

Estimation gives you a target; load testing tells you whether the system actually meets it, and — more usefully — exactly where and how it fails once pushed past that point. Different test shapes answer different questions:

Test typeShapeAnswers
Smoke test Minimal load, quick run Does the system work at all, under trivial load, before a real test is worth the time?
Load test Expected peak traffic, sustained Does the system meet its latency/error targets at the load you actually planned for?
Stress test Ramp well past expected peak until something breaks Where's the wall, and does it fail gracefully (shed load, degrade) or catastrophically (cascading timeouts, OOM)?
Soak test Moderate load sustained for hours/days Slow leaks — memory growth, connection leaks, log-disk fill — that only show up over time, not in a five-minute burst.
Spike test Sudden, brief surge far above baseline Does autoscaling/backpressure react fast enough, or does the surge arrive and leave before new capacity even comes online?

Tools like k6, Locust, Gatling, and JMeter generate the traffic; what to actually watch is the same regardless of tool: throughput as you increase offered load (does it plateau — the saturation point — or does it fall over, meaning the system is doing less useful work at high load than at moderate load, a classic sign of thrashing), p95/p99 latency alongside it (latency should degrade gracefully approaching the plateau, not explode suddenly), the error rate (a system that keeps accepting requests it can't serve in time is worse than one that fails fast at a stable rate — see Resilience Patterns on load shedding), and resource utilization at the point things go bad (CPU pegged? connection pool exhausted? GC pauses dominating? that's usually your actual bottleneck, and it's rarely the one you assumed).

! Caution — test against production-shaped data and dependencies

A load test against an empty database, warm caches, and a mocked-out downstream tells you almost nothing about production, where the DB has years of data (different query plans — see SQL & Query Performance), caches start cold, and downstreams have their own latency and failure modes. The closer the test environment mirrors production shape (data volume, index state, realistic dependency latency), the more the result is a forecast rather than a fiction.

Profiling: finding where the time actually goes

Load testing tells you a service is slow at some load level; a profiler tells you which line of code is responsible. Two mechanisms dominate:

  • Sampling profilers interrupt the running program periodically (e.g. every 10ms) and record the call stack at that instant. Low overhead, safe for production, statistical rather than exact — a function that's on-stack in 40% of samples is (roughly) consuming 40% of the profiled time.
  • Instrumenting profilers insert timing code around every function call to measure exact elapsed time per call. Precise, but the instrumentation itself adds overhead that can distort the very thing being measured — usually a dev/staging tool, not a production one.

The result is commonly rendered as a flame graph: each box is a function; width is proportion of total time spent in it (and everything it calls); stacking upward represents the call stack getting deeper. A single wide box near the bottom is the signature of one hot function dominating runtime — the thing worth optimizing first, because speeding up a box that's 2% of total width can't move the needle much no matter how much faster you make it.

★ On-CPU vs. off-CPU time

A profile of on-CPU time (what a standard CPU profiler shows) only sees code that's actively executing — it's blind to a thread that's blocked waiting on a lock, a DB response, or I/O, because a blocked thread isn't consuming CPU and so never appears as a "hot" stack. If your service is slow but every CPU profile looks flat and uninteresting, the bottleneck is very likely off-CPU — waiting, not computing — and you need a tool built for that (e.g. off-CPU flame graphs, or simply a distributed trace showing a span mostly spent awaiting a downstream call, exactly the Observability page's trace waterfall). This is one of the most common false leads in performance work: profiling compute when the problem is waiting.

1. A service reports mean latency of 50ms. What's the one honest conclusion you can draw from that number alone?

The mean is dominated by the bulk of fast requests and can hide a long tail entirely — it says nothing about p95/p99 without more information. That's why production dashboards report percentiles, not just the mean.

2. Each call in a request has a 2% chance of hitting a slow path, and a request fans out to 10 such calls. Roughly what fraction of requests hit the slow path at least once?

Tail-latency amplification: P(at least one slow call) = 1 − (1 − p)^N = 1 − 0.98^10 ≈ 18%. The naive "2% × 10 = 20%" is close here but isn't the correct formula (and diverges more at higher p or N) — the multiplicative form is exact.

3. A CPU profile of a slow endpoint looks completely flat — no function dominates on-CPU time — yet the endpoint takes 900ms. What's the most likely explanation?

On-CPU profilers are blind to blocked/waiting time by construction — a thread parked on I/O never shows up as a hot stack. A flat CPU profile alongside high latency is the classic signature of an off-CPU bottleneck; reach for a distributed trace or an off-CPU flame graph instead.

★ ML-systems bridge

Every idea on this page has a direct inference-serving analogue. Latency vs. throughput is exactly the batching knob: a larger batch raises GPU utilization (throughput) but makes each request wait longer for the batch to fill (latency) — the same trade-off as the batching example above, just with a GPU instead of a generic backend call. Little's Law sizes an inference queue precisely: at λ requests/s and W seconds of model latency (including queueing for a batch slot), you need L = λW requests' worth of in-flight capacity — undersize it and requests queue past their latency budget exactly like an undersized thread pool. Tail-latency amplification shows up when a feature-serving path fans out to several feature stores or a retrieval step before the model call — the user-visible p99 is the slowest-of-N across that fan-out, not any one store's own p99. And capacity estimation is identical in method: daily active users → inference requests/day → peak QPS → GPU-seconds needed → number of replicas, the same six-step chain as the URL-shortener example, just with a model call in place of a DB write.
