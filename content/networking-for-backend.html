Networking & Protocols for Backend Engineers

The request lifecycle spent three of its nine hops — DNS, TCP+TLS, and the load balancer — on infrastructure that exists purely to get bytes from a client to your process and back. This page zooms into those hops, because they're where the first fallacy of distributed computing stops being a slogan and starts being your on-call reality: the network is not reliable. Packets get dropped, reordered, and delayed; connections die mid-request; DNS answers go stale. Every design decision in this page — handshakes, protocol choice, pooling, timeouts — is a response to that one uncomfortable fact.

TCP vs. UDP: the two contracts

Almost everything you build sits on top of one of two transport protocols, and they make opposite promises.

TCPUDP
Contract Reliable, ordered, connection-oriented byte stream Best-effort, unordered, connectionless datagrams
Delivery Guaranteed (retransmits lost packets) or the connection errors out Not guaranteed — a lost packet is just gone unless the application retries
Ordering Guaranteed — the OS reorders/buffers for you None — packets can arrive out of order, or not at all
Setup cost A handshake before any data flows (see below) None — first packet is the first data
Typical use HTTP/1.1, HTTP/2, gRPC, databases, anything correctness-sensitive DNS queries, video/voice, gaming, and — surprisingly — HTTP/3 (built on QUIC, over UDP)

The instinct "TCP is always safer" is wrong often enough to matter: TCP's reliability comes from retransmission and strict ordering, and both cost latency. A single dropped packet in a live video call is better replaced by the next frame than retransmitted three round trips late — which is exactly why real-time media uses UDP-based protocols (RTP) and tolerates loss instead of paying to fix it. Keep this trade-off in mind; it resurfaces below when HTTP/3 makes the same call for web traffic.

The TCP handshake — and why it's not free

Before a single byte of your request travels, TCP negotiates a connection: SYN (client → server), SYN-ACK (server → client), ACK (client → server). That's one full round trip before the client can even send its first real byte. On a connection with 40ms round-trip time (RTT) to the server, you've spent 40ms and transferred nothing.

i Note

RTT, not one-way latency, is the unit that matters for handshake math — every "negotiation" step is a round trip, and round trips are dominated by the speed of light and the number of network hops, not your server's CPU. A cross-continent connection (~70–100ms RTT) pays for every round trip your protocol demands; a same-datacenter one (~0.5ms) barely notices.

Then TLS layers encryption on top, and it's another negotiation: TLS 1.3 (current standard) needs one more round trip (or even zero, with session resumption / 0-RTT for returning clients); TLS 1.2 needs two. So a fresh HTTPS connection on TLS 1.3, cold, costs roughly 2 RTT before the first application byte moves — TCP's 1 RTT plus TLS's 1 RTT. That's the "TCP + TLS" hop from the anatomy page, now with a number attached.

TLS termination is a placement decision, not just a crypto one: terminate at the load balancer (common) and the LB→backend hop can run plaintext inside your trusted network (simpler cert management, one place to rotate certs) or re-encrypt it (mutual TLS, zero-trust internal networking — more common now that "trusted internal network" is a weaker assumption than it used to be). Terminate at the origin instead, and the LB stays a dumb, fast packet-forwarder but every backend instance needs the certificate.

DNS: the lookup that's supposed to be invisible

Turning api.example.com into an IP address is a hierarchical walk — your resolver asks a root server, which delegates to a .com TLD server, which delegates to your domain's authoritative nameserver — unless the answer is already cached somewhere along that chain, which it usually is. Each DNS record carries a TTL (time to live) that governs how long it's allowed to be cached before the walk has to happen again.

! Pitfall

A long TTL makes lookups nearly free but makes your infrastructure slow to change: if you repoint a hostname during an incident (failover to a backup region), clients holding a cached record from an hour-long TTL won't see the change until it expires. This is why teams often drop TTLs to something short (60s) before a planned migration, and why "just update DNS" is a much slower lever during an active incident than people expect.

HTTP/1.1 → HTTP/2 → HTTP/3: solving head-of-line blocking

Once the connection exists, the application-layer protocol decides how requests actually travel over it — and the whole story of HTTP's evolution is about one problem: head-of-line (HOL) blocking — one slow or stuck request delaying everything queued behind it.

HTTP/1.1
One request in flight per connection at a time (without pipelining, which browsers never really shipped because a slow response still blocks everything behind it — HOL blocking at the application layer). Browsers work around this by opening multiple parallel connections to the same origin — typically 6 — each paying its own handshake cost, each serially blocked within itself.
HTTP/2
Multiplexing: many logical request/response "streams" interleaved over a single TCP connection, each broken into frames tagged with a stream ID so they can be reassembled independently. One connection, one handshake, and no more application-level HOL blocking — a slow response no longer blocks an unrelated fast one queued after it.
HTTP/3
Built on QUIC, which runs over UDP instead of TCP. Why: HTTP/2's multiplexing removed HOL blocking at the application layer, but it still runs over one TCP connection — and TCP itself enforces strict in-order delivery for the whole connection. A single lost packet still stalls every HTTP/2 stream until it's retransmitted, because TCP won't hand any later bytes to the application until the gap is filled. QUIC moves stream multiplexing into the transport itself, so loss on one stream no longer blocks the others — it also folds the transport and TLS 1.3 handshakes into a single round trip.
★ Insight

Each HTTP version fixes head-of-line blocking at a lower layer than the one before it: HTTP/2 fixes it at the application layer (multiplexed streams instead of strictly serial requests), and HTTP/3 fixes it at the transport layer (UDP-based QUIC instead of TCP). This is a useful lens for reading almost any protocol upgrade: ask which layer's ordering guarantee was creating the bottleneck.

At zero packet loss, HTTP/2 wins comfortably: one handshake instead of six, and every resource travels concurrently instead of queueing behind five other connections' worth of requests. Push the loss slider up, though, and watch the gap close — because HTTP/2's single TCP connection means one dropped packet stalls all multiplexed streams at once, while HTTP/1.1's six independent connections only ever lose the one connection that actually dropped a packet. That's precisely the TCP-level HOL blocking HTTP/3/QUIC was built to eliminate, and precisely why "just use HTTP/2" isn't a universal answer on lossy networks (mobile, satellite, congested Wi-Fi).

Connection pooling and keep-alive

Paying 1–2 RTT to open a connection is fine once. Paying it on every request is not — and it's exactly what happens if a client opens a fresh connection per call instead of reusing one. Keep-alive (the default in HTTP/1.1 with Connection: keep-alive, implicit in HTTP/2) keeps a TCP connection open across multiple requests so the handshake cost is amortized. A connection pool takes this further on the client/service side: a fixed set of pre-warmed, reusable connections to a downstream (a database, another service) that requests borrow and return instead of opening new ones.

! Caution

A connection pool is a finite resource, and sizing it wrong causes two opposite failures. Too small, and requests queue waiting for a free connection under load — you'll see this as latency that spikes with traffic even though the downstream itself is healthy. Too large, and you can overwhelm a downstream that has its own connection limits (a common way to accidentally take down a database: N app instances × a pool size of M, all opening connections, can exceed what the DB was provisioned for). Pool sizing is a capacity-planning problem, not a "set it high and forget it" one — the same theme returns in Concurrency, Threads & Async I/O.

The timeout/retry budget

Every network call your service makes needs an answer to "how long do I wait before giving up?" — a timeout — because the alternative is a thread or connection held hostage by a downstream that may simply never respond. Get this wrong and a single wedged dependency can exhaust your entire capacity, one blocked caller at a time — the cascading-failure seed mentioned on the anatomy page.

Timeouts compose badly if you're not deliberate about it. If service A calls B calls C, and each layer independently sets a generous 30-second timeout, a slow C can hold open A's request for the full chain — 90 seconds — instead of failing fast. The fix is a deadline budget: the caller decides a total time budget for the whole chain, propagates the remaining budget downward (often as a header), and each hop respects that deadline rather than inventing its own. This is standard practice in gRPC (deadline propagation is a first-class feature) and worth deliberately building even when your RPC framework doesn't hand it to you for free.

Retries are the other half of the budget, and they're a preview of Resilience Patterns later in this course: retrying a timed-out call can turn one slow request into several, and retrying blindly during an outage is how a struggling service gets finished off by its own callers (a retry storm). The short version for now — never retry without a cap, backoff, and jitter, and only retry calls that are safe to repeat (idempotent).

★ ML-systems bridge

Online model serving lives or dies by this same budget. A feature store lookup, a downstream model call in an ensemble, or a call out to a third-party enrichment API all eat into the same request's latency budget as everything else — and a p99 SLA on your serving endpoint is really a deadline budget split across every hop your inference path touches. gRPC is the default transport for a reason here too: internal model-serving traffic is service-to-service, schema matters, and streaming responses (token-by-token generation) map directly onto what you'll see in the next area, Beyond REST: gRPC, GraphQL & Streaming.

1. A cold HTTPS connection using TLS 1.3 needs roughly how many round trips before the first application byte can be sent?

TCP's SYN/SYN-ACK/ACK is one round trip, and TLS 1.3 adds one more (TLS 1.2 needed two). That ~2 RTT cost is paid once per connection — which is the entire justification for keep-alive and connection pooling.

2. HTTP/2 removes head-of-line blocking at the application layer by multiplexing streams over one TCP connection. Why can a single dropped packet still stall every one of those streams?

HTTP/2 fixed HOL blocking at the application layer, but it still rides on TCP, whose ordering guarantee is per-connection, not per-stream. QUIC (HTTP/3) fixes this by moving multiplexing into the transport itself, over UDP, so loss on one stream doesn't block the others.

3. Service A calls B calls C. Each service independently sets its own 30-second timeout for its outbound call, with no shared deadline. C becomes slow. What's the main problem with this setup?

Independently-set timeouts stack up across a call chain instead of sharing a budget. The fix is deadline propagation: the top-level caller sets a total time budget and passes the *remaining* time down at each hop, so every layer respects the same overall deadline instead of each inventing its own.
